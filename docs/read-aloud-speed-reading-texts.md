# Read Aloud and Speed Reading: Reading Text Library

**Status:** Draft content for creator review  
**Intended use:** Read Aloud and Speed Reading activities  
**Levels:** CEFR A1–C2  
**Passages:** 10 per level; 60 total  
**Approximate length categories:** 50, 100, 150, 200, and 250 words; two passages in each category per level

Each passage introduces one distinct concept. Headings and level labels are not part of the passage. Length labels are approximate categories; the activity should calculate the exact count from the passage text when showing a learner a word target. Each passage is one continuous paragraph for display. CEFR assignments are editorial drafts and should receive a final educator review before publication.

## A1 · Beginner

### About 50 words · Clouds
Clouds are made of tiny drops of water or ice crystals. They float high above us, moved by the wind. When many drops join and become heavy, they fall as rain. Some clouds look white and soft, while others look dark before a storm. Clouds help move water around Earth.

### About 50 words · Magnets
A magnet can pull some metals toward it. It has two ends, called poles. If two north poles meet, they push away. A north pole and a south pole pull together. Magnets can hold notes on a fridge. They are also inside speakers, toys, and machines that help people work.

### About 100 words · Bees and flowers
Bees visit flowers to find sweet liquid called nectar. As a bee drinks, yellow pollen sticks to its body. The bee carries some pollen to the next flower. This helps the plant make seeds. Many fruits grow after flowers receive pollen, so bees help gardens and farms. A bee also takes nectar back to its home. There, other bees use it to make honey. Bees live together in groups and work as a team. They do not visit every flower in one day. They travel from place to place, following the colors and smells that lead them to food. Protecting flowers gives bees a safer place to live.

### About 100 words · Shadows
A shadow appears when an object blocks light. On a sunny day, your body can make a dark shape on the ground. In the morning, the Sun is low, so your shadow looks long. At noon, the Sun is higher, and the shadow becomes shorter. Later, the Sun moves lower again, and the shadow grows long. A lamp can make a shadow inside a room too. Move the lamp closer to a toy, and the shadow may look bigger. Move it farther away, and the shape may become smaller. Shadows help people notice where light comes from. They also make simple games fun.

### About 150 words · Rainbows
A rainbow appears when sunlight shines through drops of water in the air. Each drop bends the light and separates it into colors. We usually see red, orange, yellow, green, blue, and violet. The colors form a curved band across the sky. A rainbow often appears after rain, when the Sun comes out and drops remain in the air. To see one, the Sun must be behind you and the rain in front of you. Two people standing in different places may see different drops making their rainbows. This is why nobody can walk to the end of a rainbow. It moves as the viewer moves. Sometimes a second rainbow appears above the first one. Its colors look fainter and face the other way. Rainbows remind us that ordinary sunlight contains many colors, even when our eyes usually see it as white.

### About 150 words · How plants move water
Plants need water to stay healthy. Their roots take water from the soil and send it upward through very thin tubes inside the stem. The water reaches the leaves, where sunlight helps the plant make food. A leaf also lets some water escape into the air through tiny openings. This loss pulls more water upward from the roots, a little like sipping through a straw. If the soil is dry, roots cannot take up enough water. The leaves may bend or look weak. After rain, a plant often becomes firm again because its cells fill with water. Different plants need different amounts. A cactus stores water and can live through dry weather. A fern usually needs a damp place. Gardeners can help by checking the soil before watering. Too much water can also harm roots because they need air. Water moves through a plant all day, even when we cannot see it.

### About 200 words · Moon phases
The Moon seems to change shape during the month, but it does not make its own light. Sunlight shines on one half of the Moon. As the Moon travels around Earth, we see different parts of that bright half. These changing views are called phases. When the Moon is between Earth and the Sun, its bright side faces away from us. We see very little of it, so this phase is called a new moon. A few nights later, a thin bright curve appears. It grows each evening until we see a full, round moon. After that, the bright part becomes smaller. The cycle continues and takes about one month. The Moon also affects the sea. Its gravity pulls on Earth's oceans and helps create tides. The Sun also pulls on the water. When the Sun, Earth, and Moon line up, tides can be especially high or low. People have watched the Moon for thousands of years. Its phases helped some communities mark time and plan activities. Today, scientists study the Moon with telescopes and spacecraft. They learn about its surface, rocks, and history. Looking at the sky can be a simple way to notice a regular pattern in nature. A small daily change becomes a complete cycle that repeats again and again.

### About 200 words · Recycling glass
Glass bottles and jars can often be recycled. First, people place empty glass in a collection bin. Workers take it to a recycling center and sort it by color. The glass is cleaned so that food and other materials do not remain. Machines break it into small pieces called cullet. The pieces are heated in a very hot oven until they melt. Then the melted glass is shaped into new bottles or jars. Recycling glass saves some of the materials and energy needed to make new products. It also keeps useful glass out of rubbish sites. A recycled bottle can become another bottle, so the same material can be used many times. People still need to sort glass correctly. Ceramic cups, mirrors, and window glass may melt at different temperatures, so they do not always belong in the same bin. Local recycling rules can be different, and checking them helps avoid mistakes. Before recycling, it is useful to empty containers and follow the instructions on the bin. Small actions at home can support a larger system. When communities collect clean glass, factories can turn it into new objects instead of throwing it away. Glass recycling works best when people, collection teams, and factories all do their part.

### About 250 words · How fish breathe
Fish need oxygen, just as people do, but they get it from water instead of air. Most fish breathe with gills. These thin, feathery parts sit on each side of the fish's head, usually behind a cover. When a fish opens its mouth, water moves inside. The water passes over the gills and then leaves through openings near the head. Tiny blood vessels in the gills take oxygen from the water and move it into the fish's blood. At the same time, carbon dioxide moves out of the blood and into the water. A fish must keep water flowing over its gills. Some fish swim with their mouths open, while others move their mouths and gill covers to push water across them. The gills work well in water because they have a large surface and very thin walls. If a fish is taken out of water, its gills may stick together and cannot take oxygen from the air as easily. This is why most fish cannot live for long on land. Different fish have special ways to breathe. A few can take oxygen from air for a short time, but they still need wet skin or water to stay healthy. Clean water matters too. Pollution can damage gills and make it harder for fish to breathe. Understanding gills helps people see why healthy rivers, lakes, and seas are important homes for fish.

### About 250 words · From rain to tap water
When rain falls on land, some of it flows across roads and fields into streams. Some sinks into the ground and fills spaces between rocks and soil. This underground water is called groundwater. A town may collect water from a river, lake, or well, depending on the place. Before people drink it, the water usually needs cleaning. At a treatment plant, workers remove dirt and small pieces of rubbish. The water may pass through screens, settle in large tanks, and move through filters made of sand or other materials. A disinfectant helps kill many germs that could make people sick. After treatment, pumps send water through pipes under streets and into buildings. A large water tank may store some of it, so homes can receive water when many people turn on taps at the same time. The pipes must be checked and repaired because cracks can let dirt enter or water escape. At home, turning off a tap while brushing teeth can save clean water. Fixing a leaking tap also helps. Water does not disappear after it goes down a drain. It travels through sewers to another treatment process before returning to nature. Protecting rivers and groundwater makes the whole system easier and safer to manage. The next time you fill a glass, remember that rain, land, workers, machines, and pipes all helped bring that water to you.

## A2 · Elementary

### About 50 words · The Big Bang
Scientists think the universe began with the Big Bang. This was not an explosion in empty space; space itself started expanding. Over a very long time, stars and galaxies formed. The universe is still growing today. By studying distant light through powerful telescopes, scientists learn how it changed over time.

### About 50 words · What money is
Money is something people use to buy and sell things. Long ago, people sometimes traded food or tools directly. This could be difficult when two people wanted different things. Coins and paper notes made trading easier. Today, many people also pay with cards or phones. Money helps organize everyday exchanges.

### About 100 words · Coral reefs
Coral reefs are large homes for sea life. They are built by tiny animals called coral polyps. These animals make hard skeletons that stay behind as they grow. Over many years, the skeletons join together and form a reef. Bright fish, crabs, and other creatures find food and shelter there. Reefs can protect coastlines by slowing strong waves. However, warm water can stress coral and cause it to lose the tiny partners that give it color. This is called bleaching. If the water stays warm for too long, coral may die. Reducing pollution and protecting ocean habitats can help reefs remain healthy. Scientists watch them to understand how seas are changing.

### About 100 words · Monarch butterfly migration
Every year, some monarch butterflies travel a very long distance. Many begin in Canada or the northern United States and fly toward warmer forests in Mexico. The journey can cover thousands of kilometers. The butterflies do not all complete the trip in one lifetime. Several generations may be involved. One generation travels south, while a later generation makes the journey north. Scientists are still learning how monarchs find their way. The Sun and Earth's magnetic field may help guide them. Along the route, butterflies need milkweed plants because their young caterpillars eat those leaves. When fields lose milkweed, monarchs have less food. Planting native flowers and milkweed can support their migration.

### About 150 words · How sound travels
Sound begins when something vibrates. A drum skin moves when a person hits it, and a speaker cone moves when music plays. These movements push nearby air particles. The particles pass the movement to other particles, creating a sound wave. The air itself does not travel all the way from the drum to your ear. Instead, the wave carries energy through the air. When it reaches your ear, it makes the eardrum vibrate. Your brain turns those vibrations into the sound you hear. Sound needs material to travel through, such as air, water, or a solid object. It cannot travel through empty space because there are no particles to carry the vibrations. Sound moves faster through water than through air, and often faster through solids. Fast vibrations create higher sounds, while slower vibrations make lower sounds. Understanding sound helps people design musical instruments, quiet rooms, and machines that use echoes. It also explains why a distant thunderstorm may look silent before its sound reaches us.

### About 150 words · Solar ovens
A solar oven uses sunlight to cook food. It does not need a fire or electricity. Many simple designs use a dark cooking pot, a clear cover, and shiny panels that reflect sunlight toward the pot. The dark surface absorbs light and becomes hot. The clear cover helps keep warm air inside, much like a greenhouse. Reflective panels direct extra light into the oven. Solar ovens work best on bright days when the oven can face the Sun. The oven should be placed safely where it will not be knocked over. A thermometer can help check the temperature. Solar cooking can be useful in places where fuel is expensive or difficult to find. It can also reduce smoke from indoor fires. However, clouds and rain make it less reliable, and the oven cannot cook well at night. Different models reach different temperatures. Families can use them to warm soup, bake bread, or cook rice. A solar oven shows how sunlight can become useful heat with a few simple materials.

### About 200 words · Map scales
A map is smaller than the real place it shows. A map scale explains how distance on the page relates to distance on the ground. For example, one centimeter might represent one kilometer. If two towns are three centimeters apart on that map, the real distance is about three kilometers. A scale can be written as words, a ratio, or a small line marked with distances. Scales help people plan journeys. A city map may show streets in great detail, so its scale focuses on a small area. A country map covers a much larger area, so roads and towns appear smaller. The same map cannot show every detail clearly at every scale. Before measuring, it is important to check the scale printed on the map. A ruler can help, but a winding road may be longer than the straight line between two places. Digital maps can change their scale when a person zooms in or out. The amount of detail changes too. Learning to read a scale helps hikers, drivers, and students understand distance. It also prevents a common mistake: thinking that a short line on paper means a short trip in real life. A map is a model, and the scale tells us how to interpret it.

### About 200 words · Composting
Composting turns food scraps and garden waste into a dark material that can improve soil. Tiny living things, including bacteria and fungi, break down leaves, fruit peels, and other plant matter. Small animals such as worms may help too. A compost pile needs a mix of materials. Dry leaves and paper are often called brown materials, while fresh grass and vegetable scraps are green materials. Mixing them helps air move through the pile. A compost pile should be damp, but not soaked. If it is too dry, the materials break down slowly. If it is too wet, there may not be enough air, and the pile can smell unpleasant. Turning the pile adds air and helps the materials decompose evenly. Meat, oily foods, and some dairy products can attract pests, so home compost guides often advise leaving them out. After several months, the original pieces become difficult to recognize. The finished compost can be mixed into garden soil. It adds organic matter and helps the soil hold water. Composting also keeps some waste out of rubbish bins. Each home can follow local rules, because collection programs may accept different materials. The process is simple, but it depends on balance, patience, and air.

### About 250 words · Weather satellites
Weather satellites travel around Earth and observe clouds, storms, and the surface below. Some satellites stay above the same region because they move at the same rate as Earth's rotation. Others travel over different parts of the planet and collect pictures along their paths. Their instruments can measure more than visible light. They can also detect heat, water vapor, and other signals that help scientists understand the atmosphere. These measurements do not tell the whole story by themselves. Weather teams combine satellite information with readings from ground stations, ships, airplanes, and weather balloons. Computers compare these observations with mathematical models of the atmosphere. A forecaster studies the results and decides what is most likely to happen. Satellites also track wildfires, dust, and changes in sea ice. Weather predictions become less certain the farther into the future they look. The atmosphere is always moving, and a small change can affect later conditions. Satellite data helps reduce some uncertainty by giving scientists a wide view of Earth. The information is especially valuable over oceans, where there are fewer weather stations. Satellites do not control weather or make perfect predictions. They provide evidence that people can use to prepare. When a phone shows a storm warning, satellite observations may be one part of the work behind that message.

### About 250 words · Bicycle gears
Bicycle gears help a rider choose how hard or fast to pedal. A typical bicycle has a chain that connects a front gear near the pedals to smaller gears at the back wheel. When the rider changes gears, the chain moves to a different pair of wheels. The size of those wheels changes how far the bicycle travels with each turn of the pedals. A low gear makes pedaling easier. It is useful when climbing a steep hill or starting from a stop. The bicycle moves a shorter distance each time the pedals turn, but the rider does not need to push as hard. A high gear makes it possible to travel farther with each pedal turn. It can be useful on a flat road or a gentle downhill slope, but it requires more effort to start moving. Riders can shift gears before a hill becomes too steep. This keeps pedaling smooth and helps protect the chain. The gear numbers can seem confusing at first. On many bicycles, the shifter moves the chain between gears while the rider continues pedaling lightly. It is better not to press very hard during a gear change, because the chain may slip. Some bicycles use a different system inside the back wheel. Whatever the design, gears do not create energy. They help the rider use effort in a way that suits the road. Learning to shift can make a long ride more comfortable and help a rider keep a steady pace.

## B1 · Intermediate

### About 50 words · Black holes
A black hole can form when a large star runs out of fuel and collapses. Its gravity becomes so powerful that light cannot escape. Scientists cannot see the black hole directly, but they observe stars and gas. These clues help them understand how black holes affect space around them.

### About 50 words · The northern lights
The northern lights are colored waves of light in the night sky. They happen when particles from the Sun meet gases high above Earth. The gases release light, often green or pink. People near the Arctic can sometimes see them. Scientists study these lights to learn about Earth's magnetic shield.

### About 100 words · How vaccines work
A vaccine prepares the body's immune system to recognize a germ. It may show the body a harmless part of a germ or instructions for making one. The immune system practices responding without facing the full disease. It creates special cells and proteins that can remember the threat. Later, if the real germ enters the body, the immune system may respond more quickly. Vaccines do not protect every person in exactly the same way, and some require more than one dose. They can also reduce the spread of disease in a community. Doctors and public health teams study evidence to decide when vaccines are useful and safe.

### About 100 words · Moving tectonic plates
Earth's outer layer is broken into large pieces called tectonic plates. These plates move very slowly over hotter, softer rock below them. The movement is only a few centimeters each year, but it changes the planet over a long time. Where plates move apart, melted rock can rise and create new crust. Where they push together, one plate may slide under another, or mountains may form. When plates suddenly slip along a boundary, the ground can shake in an earthquake. Scientists map plate boundaries to understand where these events are more likely. The maps cannot predict the exact day of an earthquake, but they help communities prepare safer buildings and plans.

### About 150 words · How GPS locates you
The Global Positioning System, or GPS, uses satellites to help a receiver estimate its location. The satellites send signals that include the time each message leaves space. A phone or navigation device measures how long those signals take to arrive. Because radio signals travel at a known speed, the device can estimate its distance from each satellite. One distance places the receiver somewhere on a large sphere. Several distances narrow the possible location to a small area on Earth. A receiver usually needs signals from at least four satellites to calculate both location and time accurately. Tall buildings, tunnels, and thick roofs can block or weaken signals. This is why a phone may show a less accurate location in a crowded city or underground. GPS does not need a person to send their location to a satellite. The receiver listens to signals and calculates its position. GPS helps with driving directions, maps, hiking, and tracking equipment. It can be useful, but a map and good judgment still matter when traveling somewhere unfamiliar.

### About 150 words · Deep-sea vents
Deep-sea vents form in places where seawater reaches hot rock beneath the ocean floor. The water becomes heated by magma and returns through cracks carrying minerals. When the hot water meets cold seawater, some minerals form dark clouds that look like smoke. These are called black smokers, though no fire is burning. Sunlight does not reach these deep places, so plants cannot use photosynthesis there. Instead, some bacteria use chemicals from the vents to make food. This process supports animals such as tube worms, crabs, and shrimp. Many creatures live close to a vent, where they can find warmth and useful chemicals. Others visit only sometimes. Scientists use deep-sea robots to observe these habitats because they are far below the surface and difficult for people to reach. A vent community can change when the heat or flow changes. It may grow or disappear over time. Studying vents helps researchers learn how life can survive in extreme conditions. It also raises questions about how to protect places that humans are only beginning to explore.

### About 200 words · How batteries store energy
A battery stores energy in chemicals and releases it as electricity when a device needs power. Inside a common battery are two electrodes and a material called an electrolyte. A chemical reaction moves tiny charged particles from one electrode to another. When the battery is connected to a circuit, electrons travel through the device and provide electrical energy. The flow can light a lamp, run a toy, or power a phone. Some batteries can be recharged. Charging pushes the chemical reaction in the opposite direction, restoring much of the stored energy. A rechargeable battery does not last forever because its materials slowly change. Heat, age, and repeated charging can reduce how much energy it holds. Different batteries use different chemicals and are designed for different jobs. A small coin battery is useful in a watch, while a larger battery can power an electric vehicle. Batteries help people use electricity away from a wall outlet, but making them requires materials that must be mined and processed. Recycling can recover some useful materials and reduce waste. Safe handling matters too. A damaged battery can become hot or catch fire, so it should not be placed in ordinary rubbish. Battery technology affects transportation, communication, and the way renewable energy can be stored for later use.

### About 200 words · Microplastics
Microplastics are very small pieces of plastic, often less than five millimeters across. Some are made small for products, but many form when larger plastic objects break apart. Sunlight, waves, and rubbing can weaken a bottle or bag until it becomes tiny fragments. Fibers from synthetic clothing can also enter water when clothes are washed. Microplastics have been found in rivers, oceans, soil, and the air. Small animals may swallow them because the pieces can look like food. The plastic can move through a food web when one animal eats another. Scientists are studying where microplastics come from, how they travel, and what effects they may have on living things. It is difficult to measure them because the pieces come in many shapes and sizes. People can reduce some plastic waste by reusing bags and bottles, choosing products with less packaging, and keeping rubbish out of drains and waterways. Washing synthetic clothes less often or using a filter may reduce some released fibers, though these steps do not solve every source. Cities can improve waste collection, and companies can design products that last longer. Microplastics are a reminder that discarded plastic does not simply vanish. It can break into smaller pieces and remain in the environment for a long time. Better choices and better systems are both needed.

### About 250 words · How languages change
Languages change as people use them. New words appear when communities invent tools, meet new ideas, or borrow terms from other languages. A word can also change its meaning. In the past, a word may have described one thing, but later speakers may use it in a broader or different way. Pronunciation and grammar can change too, often so gradually that people do not notice them from one year to the next. People move, trade, study, and communicate across regions. These contacts bring language forms together. A community may borrow words from neighbors or mix expressions from several traditions. Children also learn language from adults, but they do not copy every detail exactly. Small differences can spread through a group over generations. This is one reason accents vary by place and family. Written language may preserve older forms, while everyday speech continues to shift. No language is completely fixed. Dictionaries and grammar books describe patterns that are common at a particular time, but they do not stop people from creating new expressions. Social media can help a new phrase travel quickly, though many phrases disappear just as fast. Studying language change helps historians understand how people lived and how communities met. It also reminds us that the way people speak is connected to identity. A change in language is not automatically a mistake. Often it is evidence that a living community is using its language in new situations.

### About 250 words · Bridges and strong winds
A bridge must carry people, vehicles, and its own weight. It also faces forces from wind, temperature, water, and sometimes earthquakes. Engineers study these forces before choosing a design. A short bridge over a small stream may need a simple structure, while a long bridge across a wide bay requires a more complex plan. The materials and shape affect how the bridge moves under pressure. Wind pushes against a bridge and can make it sway. A small amount of movement is normal, but too much can make travel uncomfortable or unsafe. Engineers use models and computer simulations to estimate how air will flow around the structure. Some bridges include openings that let wind pass through. Others use cables, towers, or special shapes to reduce movement. The design must balance strength with flexibility. A structure that is too stiff may be damaged by forces it cannot absorb. Builders test parts of a bridge and inspect it after it opens. Sensors can measure movement and vibration. Regular checks help workers find cracks, rust, or loose connections before they become serious. Closing a bridge during a storm may sometimes be necessary to protect people. The famous collapse of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge in 1940 showed how wind can interact with a bridge in unexpected ways. Modern engineers use that lesson when they design long structures. A bridge is not simply a road over water; it is a system that must respond safely to changing forces for many years.

## B2 · Upper-intermediate

### About 50 words · Antibiotic resistance
Antibiotics kill many bacteria, but some bacteria can survive a medicine. The survivors may multiply and pass this resistance to other bacteria. When antibiotics are used too often or incorrectly, resistant bacteria can spread more easily. Then an infection may become harder to treat, so doctors choose medicines carefully today.

### About 50 words · Dark matter
Astronomers observe stars moving in ways that visible matter alone cannot explain. They think unseen material, called dark matter, adds gravity throughout galaxies. It does not give off light that telescopes can detect directly. Scientists are testing ideas about what it might be, but its exact nature remains unknown.

### About 100 words · CRISPR gene editing
CRISPR is a tool scientists can use to change DNA. It was developed from a natural defense system that bacteria use against viruses. A guide molecule helps direct a cutting protein to a chosen DNA location. Researchers can then remove, repair, or replace a section of genetic code. This may help scientists study diseases or develop treatments. However, editing DNA can have unexpected effects if the tool changes the wrong location. Decisions about human gene editing also raise ethical questions, especially when changes could be inherited by future generations. Scientists, doctors, patients, and communities discuss both possible benefits and risks before such methods are used widely.

### About 100 words · Urban heat islands
Cities can be warmer than nearby rural areas, especially at night. This effect is called an urban heat island. Dark roads and roofs absorb sunlight during the day and release heat slowly after sunset. Buildings can block wind, while cars and air conditioners add more warmth. Cities also have fewer trees and less open soil, so there is less shade and evaporation. During a heat wave, these conditions can increase health risks, particularly for older adults and people without cool homes. Planting trees, adding parks, using lighter roof materials, and designing shaded streets can reduce heat. Local plans should focus on neighborhoods with the fewest cooling resources.

### About 150 words · Ocean currents
Ocean currents move large amounts of water around the planet. Some are driven by wind at the surface. Others form deep below the surface because cold, salty water is denser than warm, fresh water. Differences in temperature and saltiness help create a slow global circulation. Currents carry heat from one region to another, which can affect weather and climate. A warm current may make nearby coastal areas milder, while a cold current can cool the air above it. Currents also transport nutrients that support sea life. Plankton grow where nutrients rise toward the surface, providing food for fish and other animals. Scientists use ships, floating instruments, satellites, and computer models to study ocean movement. The system is complex, and currents can change over time. Melting ice adds fresh water to some areas, which may affect the density that helps drive deep circulation. Researchers are measuring these changes to understand possible effects. Ocean currents do not control the climate alone, but they are an important part of the system that distributes heat and materials around Earth.

### About 150 words · How MRI scans work
Magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, creates pictures of structures inside the body. The scanner contains a strong magnet. It changes the behavior of hydrogen atoms, which are common in water and body tissue. Radio waves briefly disturb those atoms. When the waves stop, the atoms release signals as they return to their previous state. The scanner detects these signals and a computer turns them into images. An MRI does not use X-rays. Doctors may choose it to examine the brain, muscles, joints, or other soft tissues. A patient must lie still because movement can blur the picture. The machine can be loud, so patients often wear ear protection. Some people cannot safely enter an MRI room because metal objects may be pulled toward the magnet. Medical staff ask about implants and other devices before a scan. Contrast material may be used to make certain areas easier to see. This technology helps doctors look inside the body without surgery.

### About 200 words · Nuclear fission
Nuclear fission happens when the center of a heavy atom splits into smaller parts. The split releases energy and usually sends out additional particles. Under the right conditions, these particles can split more atoms, creating a chain reaction. In a nuclear power station, operators control the reaction so that energy is released steadily rather than all at once. The heat from fission warms water and produces steam. The steam turns a turbine connected to a generator, which makes electricity. The process does not burn coal or gas, so the power station releases very little carbon dioxide while operating. However, fission creates radioactive waste that must be stored safely for a long time. Accidents are uncommon, but a serious failure can have major consequences. Plants require careful design, trained workers, and strong safety systems. Nuclear power is one part of a wider energy system. It can provide steady electricity when the wind is weak or the Sun is not shining. Building a power station is expensive and takes many years. Communities consider costs, safety, waste, and climate goals when deciding whether to use it. Researchers are improving reactor designs and exploring ways to reduce waste. Fission is different from fusion, the process that powers the Sun. Both involve atomic nuclei, but they release energy in different ways. Understanding that difference helps people discuss energy choices more clearly.

### About 200 words · Aviation safety checklists
Aviation checklists help pilots complete important tasks in the right order. Before takeoff, a crew checks instruments, controls, doors, and communication systems. The list supports memory during a busy period, when people may be handling several tasks at once. It does not replace training or judgment. Instead, it makes critical steps visible and gives crew members a shared way to confirm them. A good checklist uses clear language and focuses on actions that matter. One person may read each item while another checks it. This creates a small pause for confirmation. If something is not ready, the crew can stop and solve the problem before continuing. Checklists are also used after takeoff, before landing, and during unusual situations. Different aircraft have different lists because their equipment and procedures are not identical. The idea has spread to hospitals, factories, and other workplaces. A checklist can reduce mistakes when a task is complex and repeated often. However, a very long list can become difficult to use. If workers treat it as a formality, they may rush through without checking carefully. Teams need to review checklists when equipment or procedures change. Aviation safety depends on many layers, including design, communication, maintenance, and training. A checklist is one practical tool within that larger system.

### About 250 words · Carbon capture
Carbon capture describes methods for collecting carbon dioxide before it reaches the atmosphere or removing it from the air. Some systems capture the gas at a factory or power station. Others try to pull it directly from outdoor air, where the concentration is much lower. Captured carbon dioxide can be compressed and stored underground in suitable rock formations. It may also be used to make certain products, although that use does not always keep it out of the atmosphere permanently. The process requires energy, equipment, and careful monitoring. A capture system attached to a factory can reduce emissions from that source, but it does not stop the factory from using fossil fuels. Direct air capture needs even more energy because carbon dioxide is spread thinly through the air. Researchers are testing different materials and designs to make these methods more efficient. Storage sites must be chosen carefully to reduce the chance of leaks. Some experts see carbon capture as useful for industries that are difficult to change, such as cement making. Others warn that it could distract from reducing emissions at their source. Building a system also costs money, and communities may have concerns about pipelines or storage locations. Carbon capture is not a single solution to climate change. It is one possible tool that needs transparent rules, accurate measurement, and comparison with alternatives. Cutting energy waste, using cleaner power, and protecting forests remain important parts of a broader climate plan.

### About 250 words · How misinformation spreads online
Misinformation is false or misleading information that people share, whether or not they know it is wrong. Online platforms can help a claim travel quickly because users can copy, forward, and repost it with little effort. A dramatic headline may attract more attention than a careful explanation. Images or short videos can also seem convincing even when they lack context. People may share a claim because it supports what they already believe, makes them feel worried, or comes from someone they trust. Recommendation systems often show users material similar to what they have viewed before. This can create a narrow information space where one claim appears repeatedly. Repetition can make an idea feel familiar, but familiarity does not prove that it is true. A post may also be changed as it moves from one account to another. Readers can slow the spread by checking the original source, looking for the date, and comparing reports from reliable organizations. A reverse image search can reveal whether an old picture is being used in a new story. It helps to pause before sharing something that creates a strong emotional reaction. Schools, newsrooms, and platforms can teach verification skills and make corrections easier to find. No single check catches every false claim. Good information habits combine curiosity with patience. When people ask where a claim came from and what evidence supports it, they make online discussion more reliable.

## C1 · Advanced

### About 50 words · The Fermi paradox
The Fermi paradox is a question about life beyond Earth. The universe is enormous and very old, with countless stars and planets. So why have we not found clear signs of other civilizations? Scientists suggest many possibilities: perhaps life is rare, distant, or difficult to detect. The question remains open.

### About 50 words · The placebo effect
A placebo is a treatment with no active medicine, yet some people feel better after receiving one. Expectations, attention, and the treatment setting can influence symptoms. This change is called the placebo effect. It does not mean a person is pretending. Researchers use placebos to compare treatments fairly and carefully.

### About 100 words · Simpson’s paradox
Simpson’s paradox occurs when a pattern appears in separate groups but changes or reverses after the groups are combined. Imagine two hospitals treating patients with different levels of illness. One hospital might have a higher recovery rate in both groups, yet a lower overall rate because it treated more very sick patients. The combined numbers hide the role of the groups. This does not mean the data are false. It means that context matters. Researchers examine how groups were formed and what other factors may affect results. The paradox is a reminder to ask how a total was calculated before drawing a conclusion from it.

### About 100 words · Methane from permafrost
Permafrost is ground that remains frozen for at least two years. It stores old plant material that did not fully decay in the cold. As the Arctic warms, some permafrost thaws and microbes begin breaking down that material. This process can release carbon dioxide and methane, both greenhouse gases. Methane traps more heat than carbon dioxide over a shorter period, though it remains in the atmosphere for less time. The amount released depends on local conditions, such as water, soil, and temperature. Scientists measure these changes in the field and from satellites. Better estimates help climate models account for feedback between warming and thawing ground.

### About 150 words · Desalination
Desalination removes dissolved salts from seawater or salty groundwater to produce fresh water. One common method pushes water through a very fine membrane. Water molecules pass through, while much of the salt remains behind. Another method heats salty water until it evaporates, then cools the vapor into fresh liquid. Both methods require energy and equipment. Desalination can help dry regions that have access to the sea. It may supply drinking water during drought, but it is usually more expensive than using nearby rivers or groundwater. The process also creates concentrated brine, which must be managed carefully. If brine is released without planning, it can affect marine life near the outlet. Some plants use renewable energy to reduce emissions from operation. Engineers are improving membranes and recovering energy from the pressure used in filtration. Desalination can be useful, but it works best as one part of a broader water plan. The right choice depends on local geography, cost, energy supply, and the needs of communities and ecosystems.

### About 150 words · Wildlife corridors
A wildlife corridor is a strip of habitat that connects areas separated by roads, farms, or buildings. Animals can use it to travel between feeding and breeding places. Connected habitat may also help populations find new areas when conditions change. A corridor can be a forest path, a riverbank, or a protected bridge over a highway. When habitats become isolated, animals may have fewer places to find food or mates. Small populations can become more vulnerable to disease or local disasters. A corridor can reduce some of these risks, but its design must fit the species that use it. A crossing that works for deer may not help small reptiles or insects. Conservation teams study animal movement with cameras, tracks, and field observations. They also speak with landowners and communities who live nearby. Roads need signs or barriers to guide animals safely toward crossings. Planning them well requires knowledge of local wildlife, land use, and human needs.

### About 200 words · Open-science peer review
Peer review is a process in which researchers ask other experts to examine a study before it is published. Reviewers may check whether the question is clear, the methods are suitable, and the evidence supports the conclusions. Their comments can help authors find weaknesses and explain their work more carefully. Review does not guarantee that a study is correct, but it can improve its quality. Open science aims to make research methods and materials easier to inspect. Researchers may share data, analysis code, and study plans so that others can understand how results were produced. Some journals also make reviewer comments public. This can show how a paper changed during review and reveal the questions that experts considered. Sharing information can help other researchers repeat an analysis or test a result with new evidence. There are challenges. Data may contain private information, and some research materials cannot be shared safely. Reviewers also need time, and public comments may discourage honest criticism if people fear conflict. A large amount of shared material is not automatically useful unless it is organized and explained. Open practices work best when researchers protect participants, document decisions, and make resources understandable. Peer review and open science are related, but they are not the same thing. One evaluates research; the other increases transparency around how research is conducted and communicated.

### About 200 words · AI-assisted medical triage
Medical triage helps healthcare teams decide which patients need attention first. In a busy clinic or emergency department, staff must consider the seriousness of each person's condition and the resources available. Some computer systems analyze information such as symptoms, measurements, or medical history to estimate risk. An AI tool may help identify patterns, but it does not understand a patient in the way a clinician does. A useful system should be tested with people who resemble the patients who will use it. If the training data exclude certain groups, the tool may work less well for them. This could lead to delayed care or unfair decisions. Clinicians need to know what information the system considered and how uncertain its estimate is. A tool should support a trained professional, not silently replace judgment. Patients should also know when important decisions involve automated assistance. Hospitals must monitor performance after a system is introduced. Patient populations, medical practices, and data quality can change over time. Clear rules should explain who is responsible when a tool gives a poor recommendation. AI can help organize information quickly, but speed alone does not make care safer. Hospitals should compare the system with existing practice and stop using it if it causes harm or fails to provide a clear benefit.

### About 250 words · Satellite-guided farming
Satellite-guided farming uses images and location data to help farmers manage fields more precisely. Satellites can show changes in crop color, surface temperature, and plant growth across a large area. These patterns may reveal where crops need water, where pests are spreading, or where soil conditions differ. Farmers can compare images over time instead of relying on one observation. A satellite image does not tell a farmer exactly what action to take. Cloud cover can hide the ground, and an unusual color may have several causes. A field visit can help explain what the image shows. Some farmers combine satellite maps with sensors on tractors, weather records, and soil tests. A machine can then apply water or fertilizer only where needed. This may reduce waste and lower costs, although equipment and data services can be expensive. The usefulness of the system depends on accurate maps and local knowledge. Small farms may not have the same access to technology as large farms. Training and affordable tools can help more growers participate. Farmers also need clear rules about who owns field data and how it may be used. Satellite-guided methods do not replace experience. They add another source of information to support decisions. When used carefully, they can help farms respond to uneven conditions and track changes in crops. Their results should be measured over several seasons, because weather and soil can vary from year to year.

### About 250 words · How public-health teams model outbreaks
Public-health teams use models to explore how an infectious disease might spread. A simple model may divide people into groups, such as those who can catch an illness, those who are currently infected, and those who have recovered. The model estimates how people move between these groups over time. It can help teams compare possible outcomes under different conditions. Models depend on assumptions. They may estimate how often people meet, how long someone can spread an infection, or how well a vaccine works. If these inputs are uncertain, the results are uncertain too. A model is not a crystal ball and should not be treated as a promise about what will happen. Instead, it describes possible paths based on the information available at a particular time. Teams update models when new evidence arrives. They compare predictions with reported cases, hospital data, and information about behavior. Reports can be delayed or incomplete, so analysts must consider data quality. Models can help decision-makers ask what might happen if schools close, testing increases, or more people receive a vaccine. The best use is to understand trade-offs and prepare for a range of outcomes. Public-health leaders should explain assumptions in plain language and show where uncertainty remains. A model supports judgment; it does not make the decision by itself. Communities may also provide local knowledge that changes how the model should be interpreted.

## C2 · Proficient

### About 50 words · Bayesian reasoning
Bayesian reasoning updates a belief when evidence appears. Instead of treating every clue equally, it asks how likely that clue would be if each explanation were true. A doctor, scientist, or investigator can use this approach to compare possibilities and revise a conclusion as evidence grows. New information can change confidence.

### About 50 words · Information theory
Information theory studies how messages can be represented, sent, and recovered. It measures uncertainty and helps engineers compress data or detect errors. A short signal can carry much meaning when its pattern is understood. This field supports phones, computers, satellites, and secure communication. Its ideas also explain why noisy channels need protection.

### About 100 words · Quantum entanglement
Quantum entanglement occurs when two particles share a linked state that cannot be described by considering each particle separately. Measuring one particle is connected to what can be predicted about the other, even when they are far apart. This does not allow people to send messages faster than light. Instead, it shows that nature behaves differently from the everyday objects we know. Experiments have repeatedly confirmed predictions about entangled particles. Scientists are studying how to use this property in secure communication and quantum computing. These technologies remain difficult to build because fragile quantum states can be disturbed by their surroundings. Entanglement is surprising, but it follows precise mathematical rules.

### About 100 words · The tragedy of the commons
The tragedy of the commons describes a problem that can arise when people share a limited resource. Each person may gain by using a little more, while the cost of that use is spread across the whole group. If everyone follows the same incentive, the resource can be damaged or exhausted. A shared fishing ground is one example. This outcome is not inevitable. Communities can agree on limits, monitor use, and create fair rules. Trust and clear responsibility matter. The concept helps explain challenges involving forests, clean air, and public spaces. It also shows why cooperation can protect resources that no single person owns.

### About 150 words · Nuclear fusion
Nuclear fusion releases energy when light atomic nuclei join to form a heavier nucleus. The Sun produces energy through fusion, where high temperatures and pressure allow hydrogen nuclei to combine. On Earth, researchers try to create similar conditions inside machines. One design uses powerful magnets to hold hot plasma in a ring. Another uses lasers to compress a small fuel capsule. The challenge is keeping the plasma hot and stable long enough for fusion to release more energy than the system uses. Plasma is not an ordinary gas; it contains charged particles that respond to magnetic fields. Materials around the reaction must also withstand heat and radiation. A successful experiment is not yet the same as a power station that runs reliably and cheaply. Researchers need to solve engineering problems and produce components that last. Scientists continue testing different approaches, while energy planners compare fusion with technologies already available. The timeline remains uncertain, so fusion should be viewed as a possible future source rather than an immediate solution.

### About 150 words · Ocean acidification
The ocean absorbs some of the carbon dioxide released by human activity. When carbon dioxide mixes with seawater, chemical reactions produce carbonic acid. The water becomes slightly more acidic, a process known as ocean acidification. The ocean is still alkaline, but its chemistry is changing. Many shell-forming organisms use carbonate ions to build hard structures. As acidity increases, carbonate ions can become less available. This may make it harder for corals, oysters, and some plankton to grow their shells or skeletons. The effects vary by species and by local conditions. Some organisms may adapt, while others may struggle. These changes can affect food webs because small organisms support larger animals. Scientists measure seawater chemistry and study organisms in laboratories and natural habitats. They also compare observations across decades. Reducing carbon dioxide emissions is the main way to slow the cause of acidification. The process is gradual, but its effects can build over time. It is one example of how a change in the atmosphere can influence life far below the surface.

### About 200 words · Forensic DNA mixtures
Forensic laboratories sometimes receive DNA samples containing material from more than one person. Such mixtures can be difficult to interpret because the contributors may have supplied different amounts of DNA. A sample might also be old, damaged, or contaminated. Analysts examine genetic markers and compare the resulting pattern with known reference samples when legally appropriate. A match does not automatically prove that a person committed a crime. DNA may have been transferred indirectly, and a person may have a lawful reason to be present. The strength of a comparison depends on how common the observed pattern is in a population. Analysts use statistical methods to estimate how likely the evidence would be under different explanations. They must also account for uncertainty in the sample and in the laboratory process. Modern software can help analyze complex mixtures, but its results depend on assumptions and data quality. Independent review can catch errors and help ensure that conclusions are not overstated. Reports should explain the limits of the method in language that courts and the public can understand. A careful analysis distinguishes between identifying a possible contributor and explaining how the DNA arrived at a location. Forensic evidence is most useful when combined with other reliable evidence. Clear procedures, trained experts, and transparent uncertainty protect both investigations and the people affected by them.

### About 200 words · Randomized clinical trials
A randomized clinical trial compares an intervention with another option, such as standard care or a placebo. Researchers assign participants to groups by chance. Random assignment helps prevent personal choice from placing systematically different people in each group. If the trial is large and well designed, the groups may be similar on average before treatment begins. Researchers decide in advance what outcomes to measure and how long to follow participants. They may use blinding so participants or clinicians do not know which treatment was assigned. This can reduce the influence of expectations on reported outcomes. Not every trial can be blinded, and some treatments make the assigned group obvious. Investigators must also protect participants, explain risks, and obtain informed consent. Randomization does not solve every problem. Participants may leave the trial, measurements can be imperfect, and results from one group may not apply to another population. Researchers analyze the data according to a plan and report both benefits and harms. A statistically significant result does not always mean the difference matters to patients. The size of the effect and the quality of the evidence are important too. Clinical trials are one stage in evaluating a treatment. Doctors and patients also consider other studies, costs, preferences, and medical history. Well reported trials make it easier for others to judge whether the conclusions are justified.

### About 250 words · Public-key encryption
Public-key encryption allows people to communicate securely without first sharing one secret key. Each user has a pair of related keys: a public key that can be shared and a private key that must be kept secret. A sender can use the recipient's public key to protect a message. Only the matching private key can unlock it. The keys are mathematically connected, but working out one from the other should be extremely difficult with current computers when the system is chosen and used correctly. This makes it possible to send private information over networks that other people can observe. Public-key systems can also support digital signatures. A person signs a message with a private key, and others use the public key to check that the signature matches. A signature can show that the message was not changed after signing and that it came from someone who controls the private key. Encryption does not protect everything. A stolen device, weak password, or careless sharing can expose information even when the mathematics is strong. Users must verify public keys so that an attacker cannot substitute a different key. Systems also need to protect private keys from theft. New computing technologies may eventually threaten some current methods, so researchers are developing post-quantum cryptography. Organizations must plan how to update systems before old methods become unsafe. Public-key encryption is a foundation of secure web browsing, messaging, and online payments, but its safety depends on careful implementation and good key management.

### About 250 words · The ethics of geoengineering
Geoengineering refers to large-scale methods intended to change Earth's climate, usually to reduce warming or its effects. One idea is to reflect a small amount of sunlight back into space. Another is to remove carbon dioxide from the atmosphere and store it. These approaches differ greatly in how they work, how quickly they might act, and what risks they create. Reflecting sunlight would not remove the extra carbon dioxide already in the atmosphere. It might lower average temperatures, but it could also change rainfall patterns and affect regions differently. If a program began and later stopped suddenly, temperatures could rise quickly again. Carbon removal addresses the cause more directly, but the amount needed is enormous and some methods require land, energy, or water. Neither approach can replace reducing emissions. Ethical questions arise because one country or company might affect the climate of people elsewhere. Who decides whether an experiment is acceptable? Who is responsible if it causes harm? Communities that contributed least to climate change may face serious consequences without having a voice. Researchers argue that open discussion, international rules, and public oversight are necessary before field experiments grow larger. Studying possible methods can improve knowledge, but research itself needs clear limits and transparency. Geoengineering is not one simple technology or an agreed solution. It is a set of proposals that require careful scientific testing and fair public decision-making. Reducing emissions and helping communities adapt remain essential, whatever research continues.
